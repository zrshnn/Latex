In Figure 3.1, the system creation process is illustrated using a modified waterfall model. This sequential approach consists of several key stages, beginning with planning and followed by system design, hardware development, software development, system integration, testing, and finally, implementation.

The waterfall model is traditionally a linear development framework where each phase must be completed before the next begins. Its modified version, used in this study, maintains this structured flow while allowing limited feedback or adjustments in earlier stages. This makes it suitable for hardware–software projects, where clear documentation, defined requirements, and step-by-step validation are essential. The model ensures that system components are carefully planned, designed, and tested before full integration, reducing the likelihood of major errors during later phases.

This procedural framework guides the researchers in achieving the study’s intended objectives by ensuring that each step is completed before moving forward. It also supports early corrections, which helps minimize development costs and ensures that the hardware and software components are aligned with system requirements.

\section{Planning}
The planning phase serves as the first and foundational stage of the Modified Waterfall Model. In this phase, the overall direction of the project is established by identifying the problem, defining the system requirements, and determining the objectives and scope of the study. This stage also involves gathering relevant information, assessing user needs, and outlining the resources, constraints, and timelines essential for system development.

Through careful planning, the researchers ensure that the succeeding phases—system design, hardware and software development, integration, testing, and implementation—are built on a clear and structured framework. By employing a systematic methodology, the study is able to conduct an in-depth examination of the research domain and prepare the necessary groundwork for a successful development process.

Following this, the System Design phase involves planning the overall system architecture. During this stage, appropriate hardware components, such as solar panels, \ce{LiFePO4} batteries, microcontrollers, and monitoring modules—are selected, along with the necessary software to control, monitor, and manage the portable energy device. The design phase ensures that both hardware and software are compatible, efficient, and capable of fulfilling the project objectives.

The System Evaluation phase focuses on defining specific performance criteria and testing the system’s components. This includes evaluating hardware specifications, software functionality, and overall system efficiency to ensure that the portable solar-powered energy system operates as intended. Through this phase, any shortcomings can be identified and addressed before full implementation, guaranteeing a reliable and functional final product.

\subsection{Return on Investment (ROI) Analysis}

This subsection presents the estimated return on investment (ROI) of the system based on a daily income of \textpeso40.

\subsubsection{Payback Period Calculation}

The total system cost is \textpeso37,570. The number of days required to recover the initial investment is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
	\text{Days to recover cost} = \frac{37{,}570}{40} = 939.25~\text{days}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Conversion of Payback Period to Years}

To express the payback period in years, the total number of days is divided by 365:

\begin{equation}
	\text{ROI (years)} = \frac{939.25}{365} \approx 2.57~\text{years}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{ROI Interpretation}

The computed ROI of approximately 2.57 years indicates that the system can recover its initial investment in about 2 years and 7 months. This relatively short payback period demonstrates the economic feasibility of the proposed system, making it a practical and sustainable investment for long-term operation.

This section outlines the system design of the proposed system and serves as the blueprint for development. It describes how the major components interact by presenting the system architecture, process flow, and system diagrams (context level, data flow, and use case). In addition, the section explains the key operational blocks, solar energy harvesting, DC–AC conversion, power monitoring, and GPS tracking, and includes the necessary design calculations to support load requirements and proper component sizing.

\subsection{System Architecture}

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\caption{System Architecture}
	\label{fig:architecture}
	\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/architecture.jpg}
\end{figure}

The Figure 3.4 illustrates the overall architecture of the Solar-Powered Rental Station with Detachable Power Sources. The system is designed to harvest solar energy, convert it to usable power, and provide access to users through various interfaces and technologies.

\begin{itemize}
	
	\item \textit{Solar Energy Collection and Storage} - PV panels capture solar energy, which is stored in a \ce{LiFePO4}  battery through a solar charge controller and DC breakers for protection.
	
	\item \textit{Energy Conversion} - The stored DC power is converted to AC using a power inverter to supply household appliances.
	
	\item \textit{User Interface and Control }- The ESP32 microcontroller connects with a mobile app via a router and Firebase, allowing users to monitor battery levels and manage rental requests.
	
	\item \textit{Power Access and Management} - A coin slot and relay control access to the power source, while an LCD displays system status.
	
	\item \textit{GPS Tracking and Securit}y -  A GPS and GSM module track the power source’s location, ensuring security and preventing theft.
	
	\item \textit{Admin Control} - Admins can monitor and manage the system through a mobile interface, overseeing usage and system health.
	
\end{itemize}

This system is designed to provide a sustainable, secure, and user-friendly power source solution for communities, utilizing solar energy and modern IoT technologies for enhanced management and accessibility.

\subsection{Flowchart}
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\caption{System Flowchart}
	\label{fig:flowchart}
	\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{figures/flowchart.png}
\end{figure}

Figure 3.5 illustrates the overall process of the proposed Solar-Powered Rental Station with Detachable Power Sources. The process begins with the initialization of the ESP32, which activates system components and prepares communication protocols. The system first checks slot availability to determine if a detachable power source is ready for use. Data from this process is transmitted through the internet and stored in a central database for monitoring purposes.

When a user intends to rent a power source, the system verifies if payment is successful. If confirmed, the solenoid lock opens, allowing the detachable power source to be accessed by the user. Once rented, the ESP32 continuously monitors the power source, including its GPS location and battery percentage, and sends the information to the administrator via SMS. This ensures proper tracking and security of the rented device.

The system also monitors whether the detachable power source is returned. If it remains unreturned after 12 hours, the ESP32 automatically sends reminder SMS notifications to both the user and the administrator. Upon return, the solenoid lock closes, securing the power source back in place. All key actions, including errors and system activities, are logged in the database for reference and analysis.

\subsection{Use Case Diagram}
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\caption{Use Case Diagram}
	\label{fig:use case}
	\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/usecase.png}
\end{figure}

Figure 3.8 illustrates how different users and administrators interact with the system. It highlights the actions users can take and the roles of administrators. The Unregistered User is someone who has not yet signed up for the system. They can create an account by providing their details, which are then verified by the system. If the details are correct, an activation code is generated for the user to complete the registration. After signing up, the user can log in to the system, but if there’s an issue with the login, an error is displayed.

Once the Unregistered User becomes a Registered User after logging in successfully, they can perform more actions within the system. The user can rent a power source by checking its availability, paying for it, and activating the slot where the power source is stored. If there’s a problem with communication during the rental process, the system handles this as an extended action. After using the power source, the user can return it, and the system confirms the return. If there’s any delay in returning the power source, the system will manage this as well. Registered users also have the ability to view their account details and rental history, and they can report any issues or provide feedback through the system. The Admin plays a key role in overseeing the system. They can manage users, including adding or removing them from the system. The admin is also responsible for performing system updates and maintenance, ensuring the smooth operation of the system. Additionally, the admin can view various reports that provide insights into system performance and user activities.

The relationships in the diagram are shown through Include and Extend. Include means that one action is always linked to another. For example, signing up always includes verifying the user's details, and renting a power source includes checking availability and processing payment. Extend means that certain actions may trigger additional steps under specific conditions. For instance, if a payment fails, it will trigger the "Payment Error" action, or if the power source return is delayed, it will trigger the "Return Delay" action. In summary, this use case diagram provides an overview of how users and administrators interact with the system, covering key actions like renting and returning power sources, managing user accounts, and maintaining the system.

\subsection{GPS Tracking}

\begin{figure}[H]

	\label{fig:gps tracking}
	\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/tracking.png}

Figure 3.12 shows the GPS-based tracking system used for security and monitoring of the detachable power source. The GPS module is connected to the ESP32 microcontroller and continuously obtains location coordinates (latitude and longitude) from satellite signals. During a rental session, the system logs the unit’s location together with the unit ID, rental timestamp, and rental status, allowing the operator to verify whether the detachable power source remains within the expected service area and to identify unusual movement patterns. If a unit is not returned within the allowed 12-hour rental periode or if it moves outside a predefined geofenced boundary, the system flags the unit as “overdue” or “out-of-zone” and sends an alert to the monitoring interface or mobile application for immediate action, it sends repeated SMS alerts at 10-minute intervals (with the last known GPS location) until the unit is returned. This tracking feature supports public deployment by providing accountability per rented unit and strengthening security against loss, theft, or misuse, since the last known location and movement history can be used to locate the unit and coordinate retrieval.

\section{Software Development}
This part provides an outline of the software development for the Solar-Powered Rental Station with Detachable Power Sources. The primary objective of the software is to manage the rental, monitoring, and control of portable power units through data acquisition, processing, and decision-making. The system combines embedded system programming with mobile application development and cloud-based data management through a real-time database, enabling reliable, automated, and secure system operation.

The software is designed to monitor battery status, track rental duration, determine location coordinates, and calculate user credits based on predefined system rules. These functionalities collectively enable automated locking and unlocking, efficient notification management, and reliable synchronization among the hardware components, mobile application, and cloud services.

\subsection{Embedded System Software Development}
This section discusses the development of the embedded software responsible for data acquisition, processing, and hardware control. An ESP32 microcontroller serves as the main processing unit due to its built-in wireless communication capabilities, low power consumption, and suitability for Internet of Things (IoT) applications.

The embedded software continuously collects data from the battery monitoring circuit, GPS module, and GSM communication module. The acquired data are processed locally to determine battery status, rental duration, and location of the detachable power source. Processed information is then transmitted wirelessly to the cloud server and mobile application for monitoring and record-keeping.

\subsection{Battery Monitoring and Power Calculation}
Battery voltage is measured using the ESP32’s analog-to-digital converter (ADC). The measured voltage values are mapped to corresponding battery percentage levels using predefined threshold ranges. Based on the battery percentage at the start and end of the rental period, the system calculates the amount of unused battery power.

The unused power is used as the basis for computing user credits, encouraging responsible usage and timely return of the detachable power source units.

\subsection{Rental Time Tracking and Rule-Based Control}
Rental duration is tracked using internal system timers. The software enforces a maximum rental duration of twelve (12) hours per transaction. Time-based conditions are implemented to trigger system actions, such as sending notification alerts before the rental deadline and recording violations after the rental period expires.

Rule-based logic controls critical events, including unlocking the detachable power source after successful payment verification and locking the unit upon return confirmation.

\subsection{Mobile Application Development}
This section presents the development of the mobile application that serves as the primary user interface of the system. The mobile application enables users to monitor battery status, view location, track rental duration, receive notifications, and check earned credits.

The application communicates with the embedded system and cloud database through wireless connectivity, ensuring synchronization and accurate data presentation.

Using Quasar it will enable the developers to create a single codebase that can be deployed across multiple platforms.  \subsubsection{Battery Sizing}
 
 \begin{itemize}
 	\item\textbf{ Detachable Power Source:} 
 	\begin{equation}
 		\text{Battery Capacity} =
 		\left( \frac{\text{Total Daily Consumption}}{\text{System Voltage}} \right)
 		+ \text{ESP32 Consumption}
 		\label{eq:batterysizing}
 	\end{equation}
 	
 	
 	This equation determines the required battery capacity for the detachable power source. The total daily load consumption in watt-hours is divided by the 12V system voltage to convert the value into ampere-hours. The consumption of the ESP32 microcontroller (0.858 Ah), which continuously operates for system monitoring, is then added. This computation ensures that the detachable power source can sustain both the primary load and the microcontroller’s operational requirements. Please refer to \hyperref[appendix:A]{Appendix A} for the detailed computation.
 	
 	\item\textbf{ Station:} 
 	\begin{equation}
 		\text{Battery Capacity} =
 		\left( 
 		\text{BCDPS}
 		+ 35\%
 		\right)
 		+ \text{ESP32 Consumption}
 		\label{eq:Station}
 	\end{equation}
 	
 	
 	This equation is used in sizing the battery of the main station. It begins with the computed battery capacity of the detachable power source and adds 35\% to account for system losses, such as wiring resistance, power conversion inefficiencies, and charging losses. The ESP32’s consumption (0.858 Ah) is also included to ensure that the main station battery can support all monitoring and control operations. Please refer to \hyperref[appendix:A]{Appendix A} for the detailed computation.
 	
 	\item\textbf{ Panel Sizing:} 
 	\begin{equation}
 		\text{PV Power} =
 		\frac{\text{Total Daily Consumption}}{\text{Sun Peak Hours}}
 		\label{eq:panel}
 	\end{equation}
 	
 	
 	This equation calculates the required power rating of the solar panel. The total daily energy consumption (500 Wh) is divided by the available sun peak hours (3.5 hours, based on PEC 2017) to determine the power output needed to fully recharge the battery each day. The computed value of approximately 142.86W is rounded up to a 200W solar panel for practical and technical considerations. Please refer to \hyperref[appendix:A]{Appendix A} for the detailed computation.

 	This equation determines the required rating for the solar charge controller. Dividing the total solar panel wattage (200W) by the system voltage (12V) yields the expected charging current supplied to the controller. The computed value of 16.67A is rounded upward to select a 20A MPPT charge controller, ensuring safe and efficient power regulation. Please refer to \hyperref[appendix:A]{Appendix A} for the detailed computation.
 	
 	\item \textbf{Breaker Sizing:} 
 	\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*.]
 		\item Panel - SCC
 		\begin{equation}
 			\text{Circuit Breaker} = 11.85A \times\text{Over Radiance Factor} \times \text{Safety Factor}
 		\end{equation}
 		
 		
 		This equation is used to determine the appropriate circuit breaker rating between the solar panel array and the Solar Charge Controller (SCC). The value 11.85A represents the short-circuit current (Isc) of the solar panel system. Multiplying by the Over Radiance Factor and the Safety Factor provides an adjusted current value that accounts for sudden increases in sunlight intensity and unexpected current surges.
 		After substituting the given parameters in the complete computation, the calculated value results in a 20A circuit breaker rating, which is the recommended protection size between the solar panel and the SCC. This ensures safe operation by preventing overheating, protecting the solar wiring, and avoiding damage to system components. Please refer to \hyperref[appendix:A]{Appendix A} for the detailed computation.
 		
 		\item SCC - Battery
 		\begin{equation}
 			\text{Circuit Breaker} = \frac{\text{Total Array Wattage}}{\text{Battery Bank Nominal Voltage}} \times \text{Safety Factor}
 		\end{equation}
 		
 		
 		This equation sizes the circuit breaker between the Solar Charge Controller (SCC) and the battery bank. The total wattage of the solar panel array is divided by the system’s nominal battery voltage to determine the expected charging current that will flow from the SCC into the battery during operation. A Safety Factor is then applied to account for possible current variations during charging, ensuring that the system remains protected from overload.
 		After substituting the system values into the calculation, the resulting breaker rating requirement is 20A, which provides proper protection during charging while maintaining safe operating conditions for the battery bank. This recommended 20A circuit breaker prevents overheating and electrical damage by limiting excessive current flow into the battery storage system. Please refer to \hyperref[appendix:A]{Appendix A} for the detailed computation.
 		
 		\item Battery - Power Inverter
 		\begin{equation}
 			\text{Circuit Breaker} = \frac{\text{Power Inverter}}{\text{Battery Bank Nominal Voltage}}
 		\end{equation}
 		
 		
 		This equation determines the appropriate circuit breaker rating between the battery bank and the power inverter. The power rating of the inverter (in watts) is divided by the nominal battery voltage (in volts) to calculate the maximum current drawn during inverter operation when supplying AC loads. This calculated current serves as the basis for selecting a circuit breaker capable of protecting both the inverter and battery system during high-demand usage.
 		After substituting the specified system values into the equation, the resulting breaker requirement is 80A, which is the recommended rating to ensure sufficient protection from overcurrent conditions that may occur when connected appliances operate at maximum load capacity. This breaker rating prevents damage to the inverter and battery by interrupting excessive current flow and maintaining safe operating conditions. Please refer to \hyperref[appendix:A]{Appendix A} for the detailed computation.
 		
 		\item Inverter - Load (AC Breaker)
 		\begin{equation}
 			\text{Circuit Breaker} = \frac{ \text{Safety Load}}{\text{Inverter Voltage Output}} \times \text{BBNV}
 		\end{equation}
 		
 		
 		This equation sizes the AC circuit breaker located between the inverter and the connected load. The Safety Load represents the maximum expected power consumption of the AC appliances connected to the inverter. Dividing this value by the inverter’s AC output voltage converts the load into current, representing the expected amperage during operation. The result is then multiplied by the battery bank nominal voltage to account for conversion losses and variations in real operating conditions.
 		Based on the system values used in the computation, the required AC breaker rating is 4A, which provides sufficient protection by interrupting excessive current that could damage connected appliances or the inverter output circuitry. This calculated breaker size ensures safe and reliable system performance under normal and peak load operations. Please refer to \hyperref[appendix:A]{Appendix A} for the detailed computation.
 		
 	\end{enumerate}
 	
 	\item \textbf{Wire Sizing:} 
 	\begin{equation}
 		\text{Voltage Drop Index} = \frac{\text{Amperes} \times \text{Wire Length (Feet)}}{\text{Voltage} \times \text{Percent Voltage Drop}}
 	\end{equation}
 	
 	
 	This equation is used for determining the appropriate wire size needed to safely and efficiently handle electrical current within the system. The Voltage Drop Index (VDI) is calculated based on four factors: the current in amperes, the wire length, the system voltage, and the allowable percentage of voltage drop. Higher current flow or longer cable distance increases the VDI value, which indicates the need for a thicker wire in order to minimize electrical resistance and heat generation. Selecting the correct wire size ensures that voltage loss is controlled, system performance remains efficient, and the overall electrical design operates safely without risk of overheating or component failure.
 	
 	\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*.]
 		\item Panel - SCC \\
 		Panel to Solar Charge Controller = 10AWG 
 		
 		
 		The first VDI calculation was performed for the wire connection between the solar panel and the Solar Charge Controller (SCC). Given the operational current and wire distance in this segment, the computed VDI value resulted in 3.52. Based on standard VDI wire reference charts, a value of 3.52 corresponds to the use of 10 AWG wire, which provides sufficient conductivity and prevents excessive voltage reduction over the cable length. This ensures that power harvested from the solar panel is efficiently delivered to the SCC with minimal losses. Please refer to \hyperref[appendix:A]{Appendix A} for the detailed computation.
 		
 		\item SCC - Battery\\
 		Solar Charge Controller to Battery = 10AWG
 		
 		
 		The second VDI calculation was conducted for the wire connection between the Solar Charge Controller (SCC) and the battery bank. Using the specified current, distance, system voltage, and allowable voltage drop percentage, the computed VDI value for this segment is 3.125. Similar to the previous result, this VDI value also aligns with the requirement for 10 AWG wire, ensuring safe current handling capacity and controlled voltage drop. The consistent selection of 10 AWG wire across both connection points maintains electrical balance and reliability throughout the charging circuit. Please refer to \hyperref[appendix:A]{Appendix A} for the detailed computation.
 		
 	\end{enumerate}
 \end{itemize}
 
 \subsubsection{Charging Time and Energy Calculations}
 
 This section presents the calculations for the charging time of the station battery using a solar panel, as well as the energy feasibility of charging detachable power sources from the station battery. All computations are based on theoretical values and typical system efficiencies.
 
  \begin{itemize}
 	\item\textbf{Charging Time of the Station Battery Using Solar Panel} 
 
 The station battery has a nominal voltage of 12~V and a capacity of 100~Ah. The total energy capacity of the battery is calculated as follows:
 
 \begin{equation}
 	E_{\text{battery}} = V \times Ah = 12~\text{V} \times 100~\text{Ah} = 1200~\text{Wh}
 \end{equation}
 
 The system uses a 410~W solar panel as the charging source. The theoretical charging time, assuming ideal conditions and no system losses, is given by:
 
 \begin{equation}
 	t_{\text{charge}} = \frac{E_{\text{battery}}}{P_{\text{solar}}}
 	= \frac{1200~\text{Wh}}{410~\text{W}}
 	\approx 2.93~\text{hours}
 \end{equation}
 
 This value represents the ideal charging time. In real-world operation, charging will take longer due to inefficiencies in the system and variations in solar irradiance. Considering the average peak sun hours in the Philippines, which ranges from 4.5 to 5.5 hours per day, a practical charging duration of approximately 3-4 hours under peak sunlight is reasonable.
 
 	\item\textbf{Energy Feasibility of Charging Detachable Power Sources} 
 
 The energy capacity of the station battery, acting as the energy source, is:
 
 \begin{equation}
 	E_{\text{source}} = 12~\text{V} \times 100~\text{Ah} = 1200~\text{Wh}
 \end{equation}
 
 Each detachable power source has a capacity of 12~V and 50~Ah. Since there are two detachable units, the total required energy is:
 
 \begin{equation}
 	E_{\text{target}} = 2 \times (12~\text{V} \times 50~\text{Ah}) = 1200~\text{Wh}
 \end{equation}
 
 Ideally, the station battery can fully charge both detachable power sources if all the stored energy is transferred. However, system losses must be considered. The average efficiencies are assumed as follows:
 \begin{itemize}
 	\item Inverter efficiency: 88\%
 	\item Charger efficiency: 88\%
 \end{itemize}
 
 The overall system efficiency is therefore:
 
 \begin{equation}
 	\eta_{\text{total}} = 0.88 \times 0.88 = 0.7744 \approx 77\%
 \end{equation}
 
 Taking these losses into account, the actual energy required from the station battery to fully charge both detachable power sources is:
 
 \begin{equation}
 	E_{\text{required}} = \frac{E_{\text{target}}}{\eta_{\text{total}}}
 	= \frac{1200~\text{Wh}}{0.7744}
 	\approx 1550.9~\text{Wh}
 \end{equation}
 
 This indicates that while the station battery can theoretically charge both detachable power sources, additional energy from the solar panel or partial charging strategies may be required in practical operation.
 
  	\item\textbf{Charging Time of a Single Detachable Power Source} 
 
 Each detachable power source has a battery capacity of 50~Ah and is charged using a 20~A charger. The theoretical charging time is calculated as:
 
 \begin{equation}
 	t_{\text{charge}} = \frac{50~\text{Ah}}{20~\text{A}} = 2.5~\text{hours}
 \end{equation}
 
 Assuming charging starts from 0\% state of charge, the detachable power source will require approximately 2.5 hours to reach full capacity under ideal conditions. In practice, charging may stop earlier due to low-voltage disconnect protection, typically when 20\%--30\% capacity remains, to prevent battery damage.

  \subsection{Standards and Guidelines Considered}
  
  The design and evaluation of the solar-powered rental station were guided by several internationally recognized standards to ensure safety, reliability, and environmental compliance. 
  
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textit{ISO 9001 (Quality Management)} – Ensures proper documentation, and structured design of the system.
	\item \textit{ISO 14001 (Environmental Management)} – Supports compliance with sustainable and eco-friendly practices.
	\item \textit{ISO 9806 (Solar Energy Testing)} – Provides guidelines for evaluating the performance and durability of the solar charging component.
	\item \textit{ISO 20653 (IP Ratings)} – Used to reference enclosure protection against dust and water for outdoor installation.
	\item \textit{IEC 62133 (Battery Safety)} – Covers safety requirements for the LifePO4 battery used in the power source.
	\item \textit{IEC 62368 ( Electrical Equipment Safety) } – Provides safety guidelines for electronic components such as the inverter, charge controller, and communication modules.
\end{itemize}
